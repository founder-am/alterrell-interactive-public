## THESIS (working, not locked)
Some names didn't need a single famous person.
They needed a community ready to claim something.
The Black Classics — Jamal, Malik, Darius, Kareem —
grew because of a cultural moment, not a celebrity moment.
Understanding which names belong in this category
and which don't is the research task.

### Q1. The 1975 cutoff: what changed culturally around that year
- Primary source: Fryer, R. G. and Levitt, S. D., "The Causes and Consequences of Distinctively Black Names," NBER Working Paper 9938 (2003); published QJE 119(3): 767-805 (2004). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9938/w9938.pdf (accessed 2026-11-05).
- Data: California birth certificates, "all children born in California over the period 1961-2000 – over sixteen million births" (p. 4). MEASURED (quoted).
- Timing, verbatim (abstract): "In the 1960's, Blacks and Whites chose relatively similar first names for their children. Over a short period of time in the early 1970's, that pattern changed dramatically". MEASURED (quoted).
- Timing, verbatim (intro): "Within a seven-year period in the early 1970's, however, a profound shift in naming conventions took place". MEASURED (quoted).
- Explanation, verbatim: "The patterns in the data appear most consistent with a model in which the rise of the Black Power movement influenced how Blacks perceived their identities." They also cite "widespread changes in hair styles and the rising popularity of afro-centric clothing between 1968-1975 (Van Deburg 1992, Woodward 1974)". Black county concentration predicts Black names most strongly "in the 1968-1977 period". MEASURED (quoted).
- What this means for the brief (not a rewrite): the primary source dates the shift to 1968-1977, peaking in the early 1970s, not 1975. A 1975 cutoff would sit after most of the shift Fryer and Levitt measured. AMA rules on the cutoff.
- Scope caveat, verbatim (data appendix): "Arabic First Names: To identify and discard Arabic names from our data, we used Bruce Lansky (1999); Baby Names Around the World." The paper's "distinctively Black names" measure therefore excludes Arabic-origin names. It does not on its own speak to Jamal, Malik or Kareem. MEASURED (quoted).
- Supporting peer-reviewed source, abstract only (full text not read): Lieberson, S. and Mikelson, K. B., "Distinctive African American Names: An Experimental, Historical, and Linguistic Analysis of Innovation," American Sociological Review 60(6): 928-946 (1995). Found via web search 2026-11-05. Its content is not used here until the full text is read.

### Q2. Jamal: Arabic origin or African American coinage
- Etymology: "Means "beauty" in Arabic, from the root جمل (jamala) meaning "to be beautiful"". Usage tags listed: "Arabic, Urdu, Indonesian, Malay, African American". https://www.behindthename.com/name/jamal (accessed 2026-11-05). SECONDARY (name reference site; no primary etymological dictionary was reachable this run).
- "an Arabic given name and surname meaning "beauty." It is popular in the Arab and Muslim worlds and among African Americans." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamal (accessed 2026-11-05). SECONDARY.
- Finding: Jamal is an existing Arabic name adopted into African American use, not a US coinage. The variant spelling Jaamal is tagged African American at behindthename (same URL). No source found this run dates when or how the adoption happened.
- "154×": the brief does not say what this ratio is measured against. PLACEHOLDER until SSA data is in hand.

### Q3. Malik: 1,659 peak (2002) and whether a celebrity drove it
- Etymology: "Means "king" in Arabic. In Islamic tradition الملك (al-Malik) is one of the 99 names of Allah." https://www.behindthename.com/name/malik-1 (accessed 2026-11-05). SECONDARY.
